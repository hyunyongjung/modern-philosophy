*First published September 28, 2026 · Last revised September 28, 2026*

> I began by asking which kind of person would have the advantage. I ended by wondering who would be allowed to use the advantage at all.

The question began with my mother.

My mother brought up Bill Gates and Elon Musk. I heard a clear message: become an engineer, or at least acquire an engineer's depth of knowledge. If I wanted to build and lead something, how independent could I really be without understanding the technology underneath it?

That made me ask a question that initially sounded like an argument about status.

Who is above whom: the engineer or the businessperson?

But I was already unsure whether the division would last. If AI could supply more of what either person lacked, perhaps choosing the right side was becoming less important. Perhaps a person with an idea could cross the boundary without first spending years acquiring another professional identity.

Then I watched a humanoid robot doing household work. It was almost my size.

The question stopped being only about careers.

It moved into restaurants, electric animals, handmade objects, inherited wealth, hidden recipes, and the possibility of having more choices while doing less of the choosing.

The inquiry follows a thought experiment: suppose AI and robots could supply nearly all the expertise that people now acquire through training and experience. What would follow? Whether everyone could actually use that capability is a separate question—and eventually becomes the most important one.

---

## Who Is Above Whom?

**Q. Is the engineer more fundamental than the businessperson, or is it the other way around?**

The word *above* hides several different questions.

It can mean authority: who can approve a budget or stop a project? It can mean indispensability: whose absence makes the undertaking impossible? Or it can mean value: whose contribution deserves more credit or compensation?

Someone can possess formal authority without possessing the knowledge needed to carry out the decision. Someone else can be technically indispensable without owning the company or setting its direction. These are different kinds of power.

An engineer might say that no strategy matters if the product cannot work. A businessperson might answer that a working product is not yet a viable business. Both have identified something the other depends on.

That brought me back to my mother's concern. Perhaps the point was less about ranking professions than about dependence: how much of my judgment would I have to borrow from someone whose work I could not understand?

**Q. But what exactly are we calling an engineer and a businessperson? Aren't both solving problems under constraints?**

They are, which is why defining an engineer simply as a problem-solver makes the category too broad. A cook, a doctor, and a manager also solve problems. Defining engineering only as writing code or building machines makes it too narrow.

Here, an engineer is someone whose work centrally involves applying technical knowledge to designing, implementing, testing, and improving systems under constraints. Safety, reliability, usability, and competing requirements matter alongside efficiency. ABET's account of engineering design includes identifying needs, evaluating alternatives, and making trade-offs.[^1]

By a businessperson, I mean someone primarily concerned with turning an undertaking into a viable enterprise: identifying whom it serves, coordinating resources and relationships, and deciding which commitments to make. Management, entrepreneurship, and ownership can overlap, but they describe different roles.

The distinction concerns emphasis, not two kinds of mind. An engineer can identify a need; a businessperson can work through its technical requirements. A founder might do both while an investor owns most of the company.

The attractive shorthand—engineers handle *how*, businesspeople handle *what* and *why*—breaks down as soon as a real project begins. What is technically possible changes what is worth attempting. What people need changes the technical problem.

**Q. Then why should technical expertise feel like an advantage?**

Because obtaining an answer and recognizing its limitations are different abilities.

Imagine two people receiving the same AI-generated application. One sees a page that opens and a button that works. The other notices that one user's request can expose another user's information. Technical understanding has changed what counts as an acceptable result.

That advantage depends on the expert seeing something the AI missed. My question was what happens when the AI can identify the flaw, explain it, fix it, and test the repair more reliably than either person.

Saying that the engineer must remain ahead because the engineer catches AI's mistakes assumes that this particular gap will never close. I wanted to know what happens after it does.

## Borrowing Another Profession

**Q. If AI supplies what each person lacks, does the boundary between engineering and business begin to disappear?**

The activities remain different. The boundary around who can attempt them becomes easier to cross.

Designing a reliable system and finding customers are still distinct problems. But addressing both might no longer require a person to complete two entirely separate professional educations before beginning.

A field experiment reported in 2025 offers an early example. Professionals at Procter & Gamble worked on product-innovation problems with or without generative AI. AI-assisted participants produced proposals that combined technical and commercial perspectives more readily; individuals with AI also reached performance comparable to two-person teams without it in those tasks.[^2]

The larger possibility is what interests me. A business-oriented person uses AI to implement a prototype. A technically oriented person uses it to investigate customers and develop a commercial proposal. Working across that boundary, either might discover that the original problem had been badly framed.

Instead of merely doing an established job faster, they get to attempt work previously outside their practical reach.

**Q. Is that what makes the one-person business interesting?**

Yes. It changes what a person can test before needing to assemble an organization.

An idea that once required finding several collaborators might become something I can try immediately. I can see it, use it, change it, and show it to someone before deciding whether it deserves a company. The distance between having an idea and encountering its flaws gets shorter.

That one-person business still depends on model providers, cloud infrastructure, suppliers, payment systems, and customers. The work has been reorganized across a network rather than contained inside a self-sufficient individual.

But from the founder's side, the change is substantial. Expertise can be learned during the attempt or obtained through a tool, instead of serving as a qualification that must be acquired before the attempt is allowed to begin.

**Q. Isn't this also the experience I want people to have in McKIS?**

It is.

What I want for our student club is the experience of discovering that something previously placed outside one's ability can now be attempted. An impressive software demonstration is interesting. Making something that answers a question of your own is different.

A person interested in finance might build a small tool. Someone comfortable with programming might investigate whether anyone needs what they have made. Someone without a settled interest might discover a question worth pursuing by trying something, rather than waiting for a complete plan.

I want to loosen the connection between *I have not trained in this field* and *therefore I cannot begin*. A prescribed list of prompts could miss the point if participants learn only to reproduce the demonstration. The experience I am after includes deciding what to attempt and what to change when it disappoints them.

That is an expansion of agency: the practical ability to act on an interest. It will matter later that receiving a powerful output and acquiring that kind of agency are different experiences.

## A Robot My Size

**Q. What changed when the same possibility acquired a body?**

A business leader I know had recently met several robotics companies and sent me names including Booster Robotics, Figure, and 1X. I watched a Figure video showing household work, and imagined the same kind of machine moving into restaurants and hotels.

One detail made the possibility unusually concrete. Figure lists Figure 03 at five feet eight inches and sixty-one kilograms—close to my own size. The company presents laundry, cleaning, and dishwashing as household uses.[^3]

This was not an enormous factory arm behind a barrier. It was a body that looked as though it could occupy the spaces in which I already lived.

A demonstration leaves questions about everyday reliability and independence. Elsewhere in the industry, 1X explicitly includes an Expert Mode for NEO in which a human can remotely guide unfamiliar tasks.[^4] So I was watching an image of a possible future, with substantial questions still attached to its present performance.

Even so, the image changed the scale of the inquiry. If machines take over physical tasks as well as cognitive ones, what remains valuable about having a person do the work?

**Q. Would I actually want a robot to do my household work?**

Very much.

Part of the attraction is that I would not be asking another person to organize their life around my minor conveniences. I could want a floor cleaned or a dish washed without wanting a relationship in which a human being is treated as an appliance.

The word *slave* came up in the conversation. What I actually wanted was a machine I could use without subordinating another person's life to mine. That attraction assumes a machine without experiences or interests of its own; a sentient artificial being would change the question.

It also matters whether someone is operating the machine remotely. If an unseen worker is still performing the task, the labor has moved out of sight. What appeals to me is relief from unwanted labor, not the invisibility of the person doing it.

**Q. Does wanting robotic help mean I would always prefer robotic service?**

No. That was the next surprise.

For some tasks, I want an outcome with as little friction as possible. I do not need the personhood of whoever—or whatever—carries an empty plate away. For other experiences, the interaction is part of what I am seeking.

A household chore and an evening in a restaurant can involve similar movements while answering different desires. Treating both as the delivery of a function misses the reason I would pay for the second.

## The Person Across the Table

**Q. Imagine a three-star restaurant with flawless robot service. What could still be missing?**

In the restaurant I am imagining, the robot places every dish correctly, explains it clearly, remembers allergies, and refills a glass at the right moment. Nothing goes wrong.

I could admire the competence and still feel that the evening had stopped at competence.

My expectation of an exceptional meal includes another person reading the table, deciding whether to interrupt, responding to a joke, or conveying enthusiasm that I understand as their own. There is pleasure in the feeling that someone has noticed the particular people sitting here tonight.

Michelin awards its stars for the cooking.[^5] My expectation reaches beyond that assessment to the whole evening. I want to know whether attention from another human changes the experience even when the movements, explanations, and timing are equally good.

**Q. But why couldn't a sufficiently advanced robot do all of those things too?**

It might. An argument resting on the robot's inability to time a joke or notice discomfort lasts only until the robot improves.

So give it those abilities. Let the conversation be just as enjoyable. Let it notice the hesitation before I answer and respond with exactly the right amount of warmth.

Would knowing that a human chose to attend to me still make a difference?

I think it would. Part of what I value is a relationship to the person who acts, rather than only the visible result. A joke can be enjoyable because of what was said and because of who chose to say it to me.

Of course, a human server can follow a script, and another diner might prefer the robot. The point is that a preference for the person can survive equal performance. The source of the attention is part of the experience being compared.

The robot need not perform badly for someone to prefer the human.

**Q. Could exceptional human service therefore become more valuable as robotic service becomes ordinary?**

Yes, where customers value that difference enough to pay for it.

If reliable robotic service were readily available, a restaurant might charge more for sustained attention from a particular human host. The labor would be part of what the diner knowingly purchased, rather than merely an input used to deliver the meal.

My first intuition ran straight from that premium to more demand for excellent staff and higher wages. There are several steps between them. A preference has to become a willingness to pay; the market has to be large enough to support jobs; and the money has to reach the people doing the work.

A growing premium niche could coexist with a much larger loss of ordinary service jobs. A higher price on the menu could increase the owner's income more than the server's. Human presence might acquire a new commercial value without providing a new livelihood for everyone displaced by automation.

**Q. Then is this a future in which people who care about their work succeed and people who do it carelessly lose out?**

That was an appealing first thought: people who cultivate attention, warmth, and skill would stand out against an automated baseline.

But a conscientious worker can also lose a job when the task is automated. A person with unusual social skill needs somewhere to practice it, customers able to pay, and an organization that recognizes the contribution. Caring about work and finding a market for it are related without being interchangeable.

The explanation therefore has to include the task and the circumstances, rather than treating displacement as a verdict on someone's effort.

There is another distinction I want to keep. A market that rewards charm, elegance, or exceptional attention has priced a service. It has not measured how much the person is worth. Someone who is quiet, ordinary, tired, or unable to perform warmth is still a person, not a failed version of the premium product.

## Electric Sheep

**Q. Isn't this close to the real animals in *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?*?**

That was the novel I kept returning to.

In Philip K. Dick's *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?*, living animals have become scarce after ecological devastation. People desire them, and realistic artificial animals offer an alternative to those who cannot afford the living ones.[^6] Yet the substitute leaves the distinction intact.

An electric animal can resemble the thing someone wants without settling what that person wanted about it. The desire might include caring for a living creature, owning something rare, or being seen as someone who possesses the real thing.

I saw the same structure in the restaurant. Excellent robotic service could meet every functional expectation and leave a desire for human attention unsatisfied. What looks like an irrational preference for a more expensive substitute may be a preference for something the functional comparison never counted.

**Q. Does that make authenticity a kind of luxury?**

It can. But *authenticity* brings several motives under one name.

I might value an object because I feel connected to its maker, because I recognize the care or history behind it, or because other people cannot obtain it. Those motives can coexist. They describe different pleasures: attachment, appreciation, and exclusivity.

The source of scarcity also matters. The novel's living animals are scarce because life has been devastated. Human service in the imagined economy could become less common because businesses choose other ways of providing it. People themselves have not become rare; access to their attention has been reorganized.

A human premium could therefore express renewed interest in people, a taste for status, or both at once. I would have to look beyond the label *human-made* to understand what was actually being valued.

## A City of Particular Things

**Q. Could this produce a more beautiful everyday world rather than simply a smaller luxury market?**

This was the possibility that genuinely excited me.

Imagine a city in which standardized goods and routine services are easy to obtain. Machines provide much of the dependable background. Against it, people develop particular things: a sauce, a way of preparing fish, a small collection of clothes, a room with a distinct atmosphere, a style of welcoming strangers.

Not everything would need to be handmade. I would not want to turn every ordinary purchase into an expensive ceremony. The attraction is precisely that reliable ordinary provision could coexist with occasional encounters with something unmistakably particular.

A person would not have to outperform a factory at being a factory. They could offer a different reason for choosing what they make.

Automated production would provide a dependable background, leaving room for individual expression. I could buy ordinary necessities without thinking very hard about them, then choose a meal, a bowl, or an afternoon somewhere because a particular person's way of seeing the world was present in it.

**Q. Would using AI undermine that individuality?**

That depends on which contribution I care about.

Suppose someone has a distinctive idea for a neighborhood restaurant but cannot also manage every booking, spreadsheet, translation, and promotional draft. If AI takes on some of that work, more of the person's attention can go toward the food and the experience.

The technology becomes part of what enables the expression.

Here, *human-made* splits into two meanings. One concerns manual process: a human hand performed the work. The other concerns authorship: a person's choices, history, and commitments shaped the result. A machine-assisted work can carry that authorship without being handmade.

Even Michelin's cooking criteria include the chef's personality as expressed through the cuisine.[^5] That gives me a useful way to describe the distinction: something of the maker can be present in the result. It need not mean that the maker personally performed every movement.

For the imagined restaurant, I would ask what the technology helped this person express. The answer could be a coherent experience that they could never have produced alone, or a polished experience with little of their own in it. Using AI settles neither question.

**Q. What has to be true for people to live this way, rather than just imagine it?**

They need more than the ability to produce.

They need time to develop the work, resources to begin, a way to reach others, and some means of living while the outcome is uncertain. Their customers also need the income and attention to choose what is offered.

A city can be full of distinctive products and still short of viable livelihoods. Everyone may be able to make something while struggling to find anyone who can afford or discover it.

There is a second difficulty. If this vision requires every person to turn their personality into a successful product, the release from routine labor has become another demand for exceptional performance.

The attractive possibility was that people would have more room to develop a life—not that everyone must become an entrepreneur, a charismatic host, or a marketable original.

I could see the city I wanted. I had not yet explained who would be able to live in it.

## Back to Expertise

**Q. Before going further, what do I actually mean when I imagine AI supplying expertise?**

The whole ability to do the work well.

That includes understanding, practiced technique, experience, recognition of errors, sensitivity to exceptions, and judgment about what to do next. Someone can possess all the relevant information and still struggle to turn it into reliable performance. Retrievable knowledge might be the least distinctive part of expertise in the world I am imagining.

This matters for the chef example. A recipe can be delivered without the recipient being able to execute it. But once a capable robot enters the thought experiment, the hands and the practiced movements enter with it. The question concerns expert performance, including the parts people struggle to explain.

There remains a difference between obtaining that performance and becoming an expert. If the system cooks an extraordinary meal for me, I have acquired the meal. Whether I have acquired the ability to make it without the system is another matter. That dependence leads back to the concern with which my mother started.

**Q. In that world, is the business-first founder with AI engineering better placed than the engineering-first founder with AI business support?**

The advantage follows the problem that remains difficult.

If the unresolved problem is making an unfamiliar mechanism work, technical depth could be decisive. If the technology is readily available but nobody has identified a useful application or reached customers, another set of skills could matter more.

The tempting prediction is that engineering becomes cheap while deciding what to build remains scarce. But that protects business judgment from the very change I am asking about. A capable system might investigate demand, compare opportunities, propose experiments, interpret results, and change direction too.

So I have to compare both people using AI on both sides of the business. The engineer gets help with engineering as well as commercial work; the businessperson gets help with commercial work as well as engineering. The question becomes what either person adds to the available system, in this undertaking, for these customers.

**Q. Could the advantage then go to the person who keeps working, revising, and paying attention?**

That was my next intuition.

Two people receive a usable first version. One accepts it. The other tests it, notices awkward details, listens to the people using it, and keeps making changes. Cheaper revision gives the second person more opportunities to turn an acceptable result into something they actually stand behind.

But hours and improvements are different units. Someone can spend a week perfecting a feature nobody needs. Someone else can recognize the mistake after one conversation and abandon it. The useful effort is attention connected to feedback, including the willingness to revise the purpose rather than only the implementation.

This is the kind of persistence I had in mind: caring enough to keep noticing. It makes sense as an advantage wherever the person's interventions improve the result. If AI becomes better at testing and revising too, the comparison moves again.

And the available time already has a cost. The person who can afford twenty experiments has a different kind of patience from the person who can afford only one failure. Even before reaching a fully automated world, my explanation was beginning to depend on more than effort.

**Q. Then can anything simply be declared the last human advantage?**

I keep wanting to place it one step beyond the machine.

First humans are needed to execute. Then to supervise execution. Then to judge the supervisor. Then to choose the objective against which every judgment is made.

Each move identifies real work. It also postpones the same question. Why should this task, unlike the previous one, remain beyond assistance or automation? Calling it *taste*, *vision*, or *intuition* gives it a name rather than an exemption.

At the same time, my reasons for doing something can matter to me even if a machine performs the work better. Cooking can be worth doing because I want to cook, not only because it gives me an advantage over another producer.

The conversation had split into two questions: what can a human sell, and what can make a human life worth living? I had started with the first and repeatedly found myself answering with the second.

## Talent, Taste, and the Human Audience

**Q. What happens to physical talent, memory, intuition, and humor?**

They face different comparisons.

A footrace asks what humans can do under its rules. A vehicle's superior speed answers a different question. Dance and live performance also invite attention to the person performing, rather than to an outcome detached from that person. Physical talent therefore has places to matter beyond beating a machine at an unrestricted task.

Memory is more complicated than a store of facts for sale. Retained knowledge helps me connect ideas, notice a surprising claim, and formulate a question without first asking a tool what to ask. Outsourcing recall can change its commercial value without emptying it of use.

I also expect people with extraordinary intuition to keep finding paths others miss. But that expectation concerns particular people and situations; it gives no reason to reserve judgment as a whole for humans. Their choices would still have to turn out well.

Humor returns me to the relationship. A machine might write a better joke, and I might still want to hear my friend tell theirs. Part of the enjoyment is that this person, with this history between us, said it now.

Some of these qualities might earn money. Others might matter most precisely where nobody is selling anything.

**Q. Doesn't taste necessarily remain with humans, since humans are consuming what gets made?**

Yes—in the sense I care about here.

Taste can mean my experience of liking something. It can also mean a professional ability to select what other people will appreciate. The second is partly a judgment task, and increasingly delegating it to AI fits the thought experiment.

The first has a different role. If I am eating the meal, an accurate prediction that I will enjoy it is not the enjoyment itself. The system can arrange the meal, select it, and predict my response. I am still the person for whom the experience matters.

That is what I mean by taste remaining human. It does not require me to predict my future preferences better than a machine. I can be surprised by what I like. I can even let a system choose for me and be delighted by the result.

But being the recipient of an experience leaves another question open: how much influence do I have over the experiences from which I get to choose?

**Q. Could human distinctiveness itself become an extremely unequal market?**

There might be strong demand for human attention while much of that demand concentrates on a few recognizable people. A large audience may want the same host, performer, teacher, or maker. The fact that everyone has a personality does not mean every personality can command an income.

Even the beautiful city I imagined has this possibility inside it. Some producers could be widely discovered, others barely visible. Some could experiment for years, others stop before their work develops. The ability to produce distinctive things does not distribute customers evenly.

This is why I cannot answer the disappearance of one kind of labor merely by asking everyone to become exceptional at another.

The question about talent was beginning to point toward something less personally controllable.

Birth.

## The Importance of Birth

**Q. If AI reduces differences in expertise, could inherited circumstances become more important?**

Imagine two people using the same capable AI. One has enough savings for a small experiment and needs income immediately. The other has substantial family capital, a place to live, introductions to customers, and the ability to keep trying after several failures.

Both have the tool. They have very different chances to use it.

An unusual skill might previously have allowed the first person to compete despite limited resources. If that skill becomes inexpensive for everyone to obtain, the remaining advantages can gain importance: capital, relationships, time, and the ability to survive a mistake. Equalizing one input changes the weight of the others.

There is a force working in the other direction. A sufficiently large fall in the cost of starting could let someone enter a field that had previously been closed to them. Lower barriers and stronger inherited advantages can operate in the same economy.

What troubled me was the second mechanism. I had imagined access to expertise as a route around an unequal starting point. Now I could see how making expertise ordinary might leave the starting point more exposed.

**Q. Isn't the deeper question who owns the productive system, rather than who knows how to use it?**

Ownership changes the relationship to the output.

Using an AI service helps me perform a task. Owning an enterprise or productive asset can give me a claim on income from work I never personally perform. Controlling access adds another relationship: deciding on what terms other people can use the capability.

A skilled user might rent every important component. Someone with little technical knowledge might hold a financial stake in the system. A third party might control the customer relationship through which both earn money. Expertise, ownership, and control can sit with different people.

A 2024 IMF staff discussion note by Mauro Cazzaniga and coauthors examines related channels: effects on labor income, returns to capital, and the possibility that productivity gains and wealth inequality increase together.[^7]

That distinction changes the question I am asking of automation. How much can the system produce? And through which relationships do people receive a share of that production? The first question is about productive capacity. The second requires an account of income, ownership, and access.

**Q. Does this mean a new political or economic system must emerge?**

My worry about birth made me wonder whether arrangements built around earning a living through work would have to change.

Suppose labor income became less central to financing people's lives. Where would purchasing power come from? Who would hold claims on productive assets? Which services would be directly available, and who would set the terms of using them?

These are different questions. Income transfers concern purchasing power. Broad asset ownership concerns claims on returns. Shared access to infrastructure concerns what people can use. Each arrangement brings questions of financing, governance, maintenance, and control; naming a system does not answer them.

Nor does the scenario give only two outcomes, perfect equality or extreme polarization. Basic goods could become easier to obtain while ownership remains concentrated. People could become materially better off while finding their relative position harder to change. Access, living standards, and ownership need to be examined separately.

Before comparing institutional responses, though, I had to revisit an assumption underneath the entire discussion. I had been imagining that the people in these different circumstances could at least obtain the same powerful AI. What if that was the wrong starting point?

## The Recipe Behind the Door

**Q. What if valuable expertise never becomes widely available because the people who possess it keep it hidden?**

Cooking made the problem visible.

Suppose there is a dish I consider extraordinary. I know the entire method, can obtain comparable ingredients and equipment, and have acquired the relevant skill. Why must I remain unable to reproduce the taste merely because I am not the original chef?

The chef's identity alone gives me no reason to expect failure. The important question is whether I actually have everything named in that premise.

Perhaps a crucial preparation is omitted. Perhaps the ingredient is hard to obtain. Perhaps a technique takes years to master, and the person who knows it will teach only a few others. What looks like an irreducible personal gift might partly be a narrow route into knowledge and practice.

This changes the source of the value. Earlier, I was interested in the fact that a particular person made the dish. Here, I am interested in something another person could reproduce—if allowed to reach its conditions.

The technology's capacity to transmit or perform expertise is only half the story. Someone may control whether the relevant expertise reaches it, or whether its performance reaches me.

**Q. Is hidden knowledge the same thing as tacit knowledge?**

No. A cook can struggle to explain the smell that signals the right moment while wanting an apprentice to learn it. The same cook can know the exact proportions of a sauce and refuse to share them. One is a difficulty of articulation and practice; the other is a decision about access.

The stories I had heard about apprentices washing dishes for a long time before being allowed to use a wok made me think about that difference. A sequence of training can build skill, safety, and trust. It can also leave the teacher in control of when the valuable knowledge is passed on. The same surface arrangement admits different explanations.

The broader commercial mechanism is explicit in WIPO's account of trade secrets: confidential recipes, formulas, and source code can derive value from their secrecy.[^8]

So I have to ask two different questions about an expert's knowledge. How difficult is it to acquire? And who decides whether I get the chance to acquire it?

**Q. Then what about reports that the public receives weaker AI than a company possesses internally?**

A secondhand report sharpened this concern during our conversation: supposedly, three internal versions of Astra existed and only the weakest had been released. I have no public source for that particular hierarchy.

There is, however, a documented example of differentiated access. OpenAI's Astra announcement says the public release refuses advanced cybersecurity tasks such as exploit creation, while less restrictive safeguards are planned through Daybreak. It also assigns Astra Pro access to the Pro, Business, and Enterprise plans.[^9]

Those are distinctions in permitted use and commercial access. They matter without implying three hidden levels of underlying intelligence. Separately, the publicly announced GPT-6 family includes Astra, Sol, and Luna, with Sol and Luna introduced on September 22, 2026.[^9] That public lineup is different from the internal hierarchy described to me.

The useful question goes beyond model names. What can a particular user actually obtain, run, and deploy? Two people might use the same brand while having different permissions, budgets for computation, private data, and infrastructure around it. The practical gap lies in the whole arrangement.

**Q. Would the owner of a superior capability always benefit by keeping it private?**

Not always.

Keeping it private might preserve an advantage in whatever the owner produces with it. Selling access might earn more. Releasing part of it might attract collaborators, customers, or complementary products. A competitor might independently develop an alternative.

OpenAI's August 2025 release of gpt-oss illustrates the public-release route: the model weights were made available under Apache 2.0.[^10] Running them still requires resources, but their release gives users a different relationship to the software from simply renting a hosted service.

Secrecy also leaves room for rediscovery. WIPO distinguishes improper acquisition of a trade secret from arriving at the information independently.[^8] A hidden recipe can be reconstructed; a private technical advantage can be approached from another direction.

The incentive to keep something closed therefore competes with the incentive to sell it, distribute it, or release it before someone else captures the opportunity. How those incentives interact matters more than assuming that valuable knowledge always moves in one direction.

**Q. Then is artificial scarcity inevitable, or just one possible form of scarcity?**

Access can be deliberately restricted even when providing more is technically possible. That mechanism belongs in the picture alongside other sources of scarcity.

Some resources take physical inputs and time to produce. Some expertise takes sustained learning to reproduce. Some capabilities are available but selectively offered. A single AI service can involve all three: expensive computation, difficult development, and commercial decisions about who receives which access.

The Astra example also adds a safety purpose. OpenAI describes the cybersecurity restrictions as protection against misuse.[^9] A controlled boundary can address risk, protect confidential information, preserve a commercial advantage, or serve more than one purpose.

To understand a specific boundary, I would ask what it controls, what its stated purpose is, how someone qualifies to cross it, and what alternatives exist. Assessing abuse requires examining those details rather than reading an intention directly from the restriction.

The important addition to my earlier picture is that scarcity can be maintained around a capability after the capability has been developed. Better technology may expand what is possible while leaving someone else in charge of how much of that possibility is available to me.

**Q. Does that make the present an unusually valuable window for ordinary users?**

That is how it began to feel to me.

If providers currently have strong reasons to attract users, and those reasons later weaken, this period could offer an unusual opportunity to experiment with advanced tools. Someone without an established organization could use that opening to learn, build, and make connections that last beyond it.

Competition, independent development, open releases, and falling costs could also expand the opening. I cannot date its end. What caught my attention was the possibility that today's practical access might depend on conditions I do not control.

There is a difference between being able to use a capability now and having a secure basis for continuing to use it. I had been thinking about what I could do with the tool. I was beginning to think about the terms on which the tool had been offered.

## Choosing Not to Choose

**Q. Even if the tools are accessible, could people lose the habit of deciding for themselves?**

They could, but outsourcing a decision can also be a deliberate exercise of judgment.

I might delegate a tedious comparison while retaining the purpose, the limits, and the ability to intervene. That can free attention for decisions I care about. Independence cannot mean personally performing every intermediate task.

The concern begins when delegation becomes difficult to understand or revise. I approve a choice without knowing which alternatives were considered, what the system was optimizing, or whose incentives shaped the proposal.

Agentic commerce puts this question into an ordinary setting. OpenAI's September 2025 introduction of Instant Checkout and the Agentic Commerce Protocol described a shopping flow in which users explicitly confirmed purchases.[^11] A confirmation is a decision, but its significance depends on what the person understands and can change.

Now imagine the agent gradually taking on discovering, comparing, selecting, and buying. I might remain delighted with its service. I might also stop encountering the occasions on which I once practiced those judgments. When am I choosing a useful delegate, and when am I losing the ability to examine what it chooses?

**Q. If humans remain the consumers, doesn't that protect their taste?**

It preserves the person who experiences the result. It leaves open who shapes the route to it.

An agent could satisfy my existing preferences exceptionally well. It could also repeatedly present a narrow set of options, leaving fewer opportunities to discover that I wanted something different.

Suppose I ask for a distinctive meal, and a hypothetical shopping agent selects one only from restaurants available through its commercial relationships. I may enjoy dinner. That enjoyment tells me little about the places I never encountered or the interests I might have developed through them.

Satisfying a preference and shaping the environment in which preferences develop are different forms of influence. The distinction matters even when I like the result.

Taste remains mine in the sense that I am the one enjoying the meal. But my relationship to the choice depends on whether I can question the recommendation, look elsewhere, and change the purpose I originally gave the system.

**Q. Wouldn't education—and governments—prevent people from losing that relationship?**

That was my instinct. The ability to form and revise one's own direction seemed important enough that societies would recognize the risk and teach people to retain it.

UNESCO's 2024 AI competency framework for students already includes a human-centred mindset and critical judgment of AI solutions.[^12] The concern has entered educational thinking. The further question is what students actually get to practice, and how widely that experience is available.

Knowing that an AI can be wrong is a beginning. Deliberate delegation requires more: understanding the purpose of the task, recognizing when a result misses it, and feeling able to reject an answer that arrives fluently and confidently.

This brings me back to McKIS. If someone learns only to accept an impressive output, I have missed the experience I wanted to create. I want participants to investigate, question, attempt, reject, and redirect. The result matters, but so does the discovery that they can act on their own dissatisfaction with it.

## Who Is Included in Everyone?

**Q. Did the conversation ever settle whether engineers or businesspeople would have the advantage?**

It changed what I would need to know before answering.

Technical understanding, commercial judgment, and careful iteration can each matter. Their value depends on the undertaking, the available system, the surrounding resources, and the people who want the result.

The inquiry also exposed distinctions the original professions did not capture. Possessing expertise differs from obtaining its performance. Enjoying something differs from predicting that enjoyment. Using a productive system differs from owning a claim on what it produces.

A person might gain extraordinary practical ability while renting every means of exercising it. Another might retain ownership while delegating almost all the work. A third might have something distinctive to offer without the resources to bring it to anyone's attention.

These people have different relationships to the same productive world. Comparing their professional backgrounds alone leaves much of the explanation out.

**Q. What happened to the beautiful city?**

I still want to live in it.

I want the ordinary necessities to be easy to obtain and the particular things to have room to develop. I want a person with an idea for a meal, an object, or a place to be able to make it real without first becoming an expert in everything around it.

But producing something has to lead to customers who can pay, and the producer needs continued access to the means of making it. The ability to fill a city with things worth experiencing leaves the question of who can participate in making and enjoying them.

There is also a difference between making room for individuality and requiring everyone to sell it. The person who never develops a marketable specialty still has a life to live. A vision of freedom from routine work becomes narrower if it only makes room for successful creators.

The early excitement and the later concern belong together. I can see what the tools might make possible. I can also see that the opportunity to live that way depends on relationships the tools themselves do not settle.

**Q. Where does that leave the question that started with my mother?**

Closer to it than I expected.

The concern I heard from her was about depending on knowledge I did not possess. My answer was that AI might let me cross that boundary without first becoming the kind of expert she had in mind.

The tool can genuinely enlarge what I can attempt while changing whom I depend on. My mother's question survives, but the person whose knowledge I borrow may now be an organization whose system I access.

At first, I treated *can* as a statement about ability. Can I build it? Can I understand it? Can I improve it?

By the end, the same word carried other conditions. Can I afford to try? Can I reach the capability? Can I keep using it? Can I refuse its recommendation? Can I change direction without losing the means to act?

I still want the world in which more people can make things they once thought impossible. But the promise now requires another question.

When we say that everyone can, who have we included in *everyone*?
