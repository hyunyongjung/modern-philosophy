*First published August 14, 2026 · Last revised August 14, 2026*

> A bubble is not simply empty. It is emptiness given shape by a thin membrane. But modern debt may not be one bubble at all. It may be foam.

The first image that came to mind was a soap bubble.

Not an economic bubble. Not a housing bubble. Not a stock market bubble. Just the kind of bubble I used to blow as a child: a small, trembling sphere drifting through the air, transparent enough to seem almost unreal, but visible enough to catch light.

A soap bubble is strange because it looks like something and almost nothing at the same time. It has a surface. It reflects color. It occupies space. It moves. It can be chased, touched, and destroyed. Yet when it bursts, almost nothing remains.

That was the first thought.

A bubble is emptiness that has gained a shape.

But the more I thought about it, the more the image became unstable. A bubble is not held together by what fills it. It is held together by the thinness around what fills it. The membrane is the thing that lets the empty space become visible.

Then the question changed.

If a bubble is emptiness given shape by a membrane, what happens when the membrane is made of trust?

That is how the question moved from soap bubbles to public debt.

---

## A Membrane Around Emptiness

**Q. What is a bubble?**

A bubble is a volume of gas held inside a thin surrounding film.

That is the simple physical answer. A soap bubble floating in air is not merely air. The air by itself has no stable visible boundary. Nor is it merely water. The watery film by itself would collapse or flow away. A bubble exists only when the gas and the film form a temporary structure together.

The inside gives the bubble volume. The membrane gives it shape.

Without the gas, there is no inflated form. Without the membrane, there is no boundary. The bubble is therefore not a substance in the ordinary sense. It is an arrangement.

This already makes it philosophically interesting.

A stone has form because its material resists deformation. A metal bowl has form because its substance has been shaped and hardened. A bubble has form because a thin surface remains unbroken around something that cannot hold a shape by itself.

A bubble is not fullness.

It is bounded emptiness.

**Q. So is a bubble empty?**

It contains gas, and gas is not nothing.

But compared with the solid objects we normally imagine when we think of form, the bubble feels almost empty. Its content does not explain its shape. The air inside does not decide to become a sphere. The membrane makes the air appear as a bounded thing.

So the emptiness of the bubble is not literal nothingness. It is a lack of solid interior.

The bubble has an inside, but not a solid core. It has a boundary, but not a thick body. It exists, but not with the confidence of a stone, a table, or a wall.

That is why the most compact definition still feels right:

> A bubble is emptiness that has gained shape.

The phrase does not mean that the bubble contains nothing. It means that what appears as a thing is mostly a boundary event. The form is real, but it is not backed by a dense interior.

**Q. Is the membrane just the outside of the bubble?**

In a single soap bubble, the membrane is the condition of the bubble's existence.

It is not decoration. It is not a wrapper placed around something already solid. It is the structure that allows the bubble to appear as one thing at all.

This matters because the membrane is both the bubble's strength and its weakness. It holds the form together, but it also reveals how little is needed for the form to disappear. A finger, a dry surface, a sharp edge, a change in pressure, or the gradual thinning of the film can end the bubble.

The same feature that gives the bubble its form also makes the bubble fragile.

That is the first paradox of the bubble:

> The bubble is protected by the very thing that exposes it.

---

## Fragility

**Q. Why does a bubble feel so fragile?**

Because its form depends on an extremely thin structure remaining continuous.

A solid object can lose a small piece and still remain mostly itself. A wooden table with a scratch is still a table. A stone with a chipped edge is still a stone. But a bubble cannot tolerate the same kind of local failure. If the membrane breaks in the wrong way, the bubble does not become a damaged bubble. It disappears as a bubble.

This gives the bubble a peculiar kind of existence. It is not simply weak. It is conditional.

It exists only while a certain tension is maintained.

Surface tension helps the film hold together and minimize area. The pressure difference across a curved interface is related to surface tension and curvature; for thin soap films, the presence of two interfaces matters in the pressure relation.[^1] But even without the equation, the everyday intuition is clear. The bubble's beauty is produced by a condition that cannot be taken for granted.

A bubble is not a stable possession of matter.

It is an event of balance.

**Q. Is a smaller bubble more stable than a larger bubble?**

My first instinct was yes. Smaller bubbles look less ambitious. They contain less air. They seem easier to sustain than one enormous bubble stretched to its limit.

The instinct does not survive contact with the physics, because stability turns out to depend on which question is being asked.

If the question is about surface area, one large spherical bubble can be more efficient than many small bubbles holding the same total volume of gas. A sphere minimizes surface area for a given volume, and making surface requires energy.

If the question is about internal pressure, small bubbles are not automatically safer. Smaller bubbles have higher curvature, and higher curvature is associated with higher pressure difference. In many foams, gas can diffuse from smaller, higher-curvature bubbles toward larger, lower-curvature bubbles, causing the smaller bubbles to shrink or disappear while larger bubbles grow. This aging process is often discussed as coarsening or Ostwald ripening.[^2]

If the question is about failure, however, many small bubbles can be more resilient than one large bubble. A single large bubble may vanish when one part of its membrane breaks. A cluster of smaller bubbles may survive local failure by rearranging itself.

So there is no universal answer.

One large bubble may be efficient. Many small bubbles may be distributive. A foam may be resilient in one sense and contagious in another.

This distinction becomes important later.

**Q. What exactly is the difference between a bubble and foam?**

A bubble is one bounded volume.

Foam is many bubbles packed together.

In a lone soap bubble, the membrane separates inside from outside. In foam, bubbles do not simply float independently. They touch, deform, press against one another, and form shared films between neighboring cells.

Foam is therefore not merely a collection of bubbles. It is a structure made of many bubbles whose boundaries interact.

This changes the meaning of the membrane.

In a single bubble, the membrane is the edge of one thing.

In foam, the membrane is also a relation between things.

## The Economic Bubble

**Q. Why do we use the word "bubble" for markets?**

Because markets can also produce forms that seem real while they last and vanish when belief breaks.

In economics, an asset price bubble is usually described as a situation in which the market price of an asset rises significantly above the level justified by economic fundamentals for a sustained period.[^3] The important point is not merely that the price rises. Prices can rise for good reasons. A company may become more profitable. Land may become more useful. A technology may become more valuable.

The bubble begins when the price is held up less by underlying value than by the expectation that others will keep believing in the price.

The membrane is no longer soap.

It is expectation.

A stock market bubble is not empty in the sense of being completely fake. Real money is paid. Real people buy and sell. Real companies may exist underneath. Real excitement gathers around the asset. But the shape of the price can become larger than the thing that supports it.

That is why the metaphor works.

The bubble is not pure nothingness. It is a real form created around insufficient substance.

**Q. Does calling something a bubble mean it is worthless?**

This is where the metaphor starts to mislead, and the confusion is worth naming precisely.

A bubble is not the same as a fraud, though fraud can grow inside bubbles. A bubble is not the same as fiction, though stories can inflate it. A bubble is not the same as uselessness, though useless things can become overvalued.

A bubble is a mismatch between form and support.

The visible structure becomes larger, brighter, and more socially convincing than the underlying conditions can justify.

In that sense, a bubble is not simply a lie. It is a form that has outrun its foundation.

**Q. Then is every expectation a bubble?**

Human beings cannot live without expectation.

A student studies because the future may reward the work. A company invests because a product may succeed. A government builds infrastructure because future citizens may benefit. A family takes a mortgage because future income may pay for present shelter.

Expectation is not the problem.

The problem begins when expectation becomes self-supporting.

If I believe something because I have reasons, that is ordinary judgment. If I believe something mainly because others believe it, and they believe it because still others believe it, belief begins to function like a membrane around air.

It can hold for a while.

It can even create real effects while it holds.

But once the membrane tears, the form may disappear faster than the participants expected.

## Debt as the Shape of the Future

**Q. Why did public debt make me think about bubbles?**

Because the numbers felt too large to remain ordinary.

Korea's national debt projections move into the thousands of trillions of won. One report citing the government's 2025–2029 fiscal management plan described national debt rising from 1,415.2 trillion won in 2026 to 1,788.9 trillion won by 2029.[^4] Global public debt reached a record $102 trillion in 2024, according to UNCTAD.[^5] The IMF's April 2026 Fiscal Monitor stated that global public debt rose to just under 94 percent of GDP in 2025 and is projected to reach 100 percent by 2029.[^6] U.S. federal debt appears as a number around $39 trillion in early 2026 data.[^7]

Numbers at that scale no longer feel like ordinary accounting.

They begin to feel metaphysical.

Where is this money? Who owns it? Can it ever be repaid? If everyone owes, then to whom is everyone indebted? If the total is larger than what seems physically present, is the whole structure a bubble?

The question was not only financial.

It was about the way the future becomes an object in the present.

**Q. What is public debt, fundamentally?**

Public debt is a claim on the future.

More precisely, it is a present financial instrument backed by the expectation that a government will be able to service its obligations through future taxes, growth, refinancing, inflation, spending restraint, or monetary and institutional credibility.

A government bond is not a pile of money sitting in a vault. It is a promise structured into a tradable form. The government receives money now. The bondholder receives a claim on future payment.

The debt therefore brings the future into the present.

A road may be built before the taxes that ultimately pay for it are collected. A war may be financed before its costs are politically absorbed. A recession may be softened before growth returns. A pension system may promise payments long before the workers who fund them have earned their wages.

Public debt is one of the ways a society makes future time usable now.

That does not make it automatically bad. The IMF describes public or sovereign debt as an important way for governments to finance investment in growth and development, while also emphasizing that the debt burden must remain sustainable.[^8]

The danger is not that the future is used.

The danger is that the future is used without being enlarged.

**Q. Does all public debt need to be paid back completely?**

A household loan feels like a path toward zero: borrow, repay, finish.

Sovereign debt rarely works that way. Governments often roll over debt by issuing new debt to repay maturing debt. The question is not whether every bond ever issued is paid off from a single pile of saved cash. The question is whether the government can continue servicing and refinancing its debt without losing credibility or forcing destructive adjustments.

This is why "Can the world repay $102 trillion?" is not quite the right question.

The modern fiscal system is not designed around a final day when all public debt disappears. If every government bond vanished, the world would not simply become cleaner. Many institutions that rely on government debt as assets—banks, pension funds, insurers, central banks, foreign reserve managers, households—would lose a major part of the financial structure they use to store value, manage risk, and price other assets.

One side's debt is another side's asset.

That sentence is simple, but it changes the entire picture.

A government bond is a liability for the issuer and an asset for the holder. The same object has two faces. It is a burden from one direction and security from another.

That is why public debt is not merely a problem of "too much money owed." It is a system of promises distributed across society.

**Q. Then is public debt a bubble?**

Public debt can finance real investment, stabilize economies, respond to crises, and distribute costs across time. Debt is not false merely because it depends on the future. So the flat claim—"public debt is a bubble"—is too crude to keep.

But public debt has a bubble-like structure.

It turns something not yet present into a present form. Future tax capacity, future productivity, future political order, future monetary credibility, and future investor trust become visible today as bonds, yields, debt ratios, ratings, and balance-sheet entries.

So the better statement is this:

> Public debt itself is not necessarily a bubble. The belief that public debt can expand indefinitely without consequences can become a bubble.

That distinction matters.

Debt can be a bridge between present need and future capacity. But debt can also become a way of preserving the present by silently assigning costs to the future.

Good debt enlarges the future it borrows from.

Bad debt consumes the future before it arrives.

## Institutionalized Foam

**Q. If public debt is not simply a bubble, what is it?**

It may be an institutionalized bubble-like structure.

That phrase sounds contradictory, but the contradiction is the point. Institutions are built to stabilize things that would otherwise be fragile. A bank account is trust given administrative form. A currency is belief stabilized by law, taxation, central banking, habit, and force. A bond market is a vast architecture for pricing promises.

Modern finance does not eliminate fragility.

It organizes it.

A government bond is fragile because it depends on future payment. It is stable because legal, political, monetary, and institutional systems support that expectation. The membrane is not imaginary. It is made of laws, tax systems, central banks, markets, settlement systems, and collective memory.

That is why public debt can look solid for decades.

The membrane is thin, but heavily maintained.

And that phrase deserves suspicion rather than comfort. A maintained membrane and a solid interior look identical from the outside. Both hold. Both carry weight. Both let people build on top of them. The difference only appears when maintenance stops—which means the difference is invisible during precisely the period when it matters most.

**Q. What makes the membrane tear?**

The membrane of public debt can tear when trust in future serviceability breaks.

This can happen through many routes: rising interest costs, weak growth, currency depreciation, political paralysis, war, banking crises, inflation, external debt pressures, or investor panic. Often, the problem is not the absolute amount of debt alone. It is whether the debt can continue to be serviced under changing conditions.

A country that borrows in its own currency and has deep domestic institutions is not in the same position as a country that borrows heavily in foreign currency. A country whose bonds are held mostly by domestic institutions faces different pressures from one dependent on foreign investors. A reserve-currency issuer is not the same as a small open economy.

The same debt ratio can mean different things in different membranes.

That is one reason public debt cannot be understood only by size. A huge bubble with a strong membrane may last longer than a smaller bubble under harsher pressure. A smaller bubble attached to many unstable surfaces may be more vulnerable than it appears.

**Q. Can institutions like the IMF stop the bubble from bursting?**

They can reinforce or repair a membrane. They cannot abolish the fact that it is a membrane.

The IMF helps countries identify sovereign debt risks, provides policy advice, supports debt management capacity, and may provide financing when countries face distress. Where debt becomes unsustainable, the IMF can support a restructuring process, though the country itself decides whether to negotiate with creditors.[^8]

But there is no world government that simply pays off all public debt, and no single global membrane that can be replaced from outside the system. Emergency lenders, restructuring frameworks, and central banks are repair mechanisms operating inside the structure they are repairing.

They manage the membrane.

They do not turn the bubble into stone.

## From Bubble to Foam

**Q. Why is one bubble not enough as a metaphor?**

Because the world is not made of isolated national bubbles.

My first image was a soap bubble floating alone. That image made sense when I was thinking about a single inflated form. A housing market can look like one bubble. A stock can look like one bubble. A country's debt can look like one bubble.

But public debt does not exist in isolation.

Countries borrow from domestic savers, foreign investors, banks, pension funds, insurers, central banks, and other institutions. Banks hold government bonds. Governments rescue banks. Central banks influence bond markets. Exchange rates change debt burdens. U.S. Treasury securities affect global collateral systems. One country's "safe asset" can be another institution's balance-sheet foundation.

The bubbles touch.

So the better image is not a single soap bubble.

It is foam.

**Q. What changes when we move from bubble to foam?**

The membrane changes meaning.

In a single bubble, the membrane separates inside from outside. It creates a world by enclosing it.

In foam, neighboring bubbles press against one another. Between two bubbles there is a shared thin film. More precisely, it is one liquid film with two surfaces, one facing each bubble. It is not two completely separate walls pasted together, but neither is it a one-sided surface. It is a shared structure with two faces.

That distinction is essential.

The membrane is one wall in the structure, but it is experienced differently from each side.

For bubble A, it is A's boundary. For bubble B, it is B's boundary. For the foam, it is their relation.

This is one of the most important discoveries of the metaphor:

> In foam, a boundary is not only what separates. It is also what connects.

**Q. How does that apply to debt?**

Debt and asset are often two sides of the same membrane.

A government bond is debt for the government. It is an asset for the holder. The same financial object is experienced differently depending on which side of the membrane one occupies.

For the state, it is an obligation. For the pension fund, it is security. For the bank, it may be collateral. For the foreign central bank, it may be reserve management. For the citizen, it may be future taxation. For the investor, it may be income.

This is why public debt cannot be understood only as a pile of burdens. It is also a pile of claims. It is not one thing viewed from nowhere. It is a shared membrane viewed from many sides.

The philosophical point is sharper than the accounting point:

> Debt and asset are not two unrelated objects. They are two surfaces of one shared promise.

**Q. If one bubble bursts in foam, does the whole foam collapse?**

Foam can absorb a local failure, and that is precisely why it is the better metaphor. A single bubble vanishes when its membrane tears. A bubble inside foam may collapse, merge, drain, or disappear while the surrounding structure rearranges around the gap.

But surviving is not the same as being unharmed.

A local rupture changes the pressure distribution. Neighboring films move. Some bubbles grow. Others shrink. The structure reorganizes. In physical foams, smaller high-curvature bubbles can lose gas to larger low-curvature bubbles; in financial systems, capital can flee weaker regions and move toward stronger ones. The analogy is not exact, but it is illuminating.

During stress, money often moves from weaker membranes to stronger membranes.

From risky debt to safe debt. From weaker currencies to stronger currencies. From peripheral systems to central systems. From local promises to global collateral.

The foam does not simply explode.

It redistributes pressure.

**Q. Does foam make the system more stable or more dangerous?**

Compartmentalization is what makes foam safer than one giant bubble. Shared walls are what make it more dangerous. But these are not two separate features that happen to coexist. They are one feature described from two directions.

The thing that keeps a local failure local is the same thing that transmits pressure to the neighbor: the wall between them. Remove the wall and there is no compartment. Keep the wall and there is a channel.

Different countries have different currencies, institutions, tax systems, maturities, investor bases, and political capacities. The world is not one identical fiscal object, and that difference is protective. But the surfaces that mark the differences—banks, bond markets, exchange rates, trade, collateral, funding costs, credit ratings, investor psychology—are also the surfaces along which a shock travels.

The system's stability comes from dispersion.

The system's danger comes from connection.

That is the paradox of modern finance, and it does not resolve:

> The world is safer because risks are distributed. The world is more dangerous because risks are connected.

## The Shared Membrane

**Q. When two bubbles touch, is there one membrane or two?**

There is one shared film with two surfaces.

This distinction matters because "sharing a membrane" can be misleading if it sounds as though the two bubbles literally lose their own boundaries. They do not. Each bubble still has a side of the film. But the film between them is not simply A's wall plus B's wall as two separate objects. It is one thin structure acting as the boundary for both.

A simple analogy is an apartment wall.

There is one wall between two rooms. But one face belongs to room A, and the other face belongs to room B. The wall is not two separate walls. It is one partition with two sides.

Foam makes this physical.

Society makes it institutional.

Debt makes it financial.

**Q. Why is this important philosophically?**

Because it changes what a boundary means.

In ordinary thinking, a boundary separates me from what is not me. It marks the limit of an object. It says: this is inside; that is outside.

But in foam, the boundary is not only the edge of one thing. It is the contact surface between two things. It separates them, but it also binds them into the same structure.

This means a boundary can be both division and relation.

The membrane between bubbles is where one bubble ends and another begins. But it is also the place where pressure is transmitted. It is where difference becomes contact.

That is a better image of modern systems than isolated spheres.

A nation seems bounded by territory, law, currency, and citizenship. But its debt is held by others. Its banks hold other promises. Its trade depends on other demand. Its currency responds to other currencies. Its monetary policy is constrained by global conditions. Its future has already been partially sold, insured, rated, hedged, and purchased by others.

The boundary remains.

But it is not pure separation.

**Q. Is public debt a national problem or a global one?**

Sovereign debt is national in origin and global in membrane.

Each state issues its own debt under its own political conditions. The state taxes its people, spends within its institutions, and makes promises in its own name. That part is unmistakably national.

But the holders, consequences, and signals of that debt are often not. A country's bonds may be held by foreign investors. Its banks may depend on international funding. Its currency may respond to global interest rates. Its crisis may cause investors to reevaluate similar countries. Its restructuring may affect global portfolios.

So the question contains a false split. The origin and the membrane are not the same location, and asking which one is "really" the problem assumes they can be separated.

A country is not merely a bubble floating beside other bubbles. It is a cell in a larger foam of obligations, assets, trust, and future claims.

**Q. What is the central bubble in the foam?**

In the present global system, the U.S. dollar and U.S. Treasury market occupy a special position.

This does not mean the United States is free from debt risk. It means its debt also functions as a central asset for others. U.S. Treasury securities are not only American liabilities; they are also widely used as safe assets, collateral, reserve holdings, and benchmarks.[^9]

The central bubble is therefore strange.

It is one of the largest bubbles, but also one of the membranes that other bubbles lean on.

That makes its stability unusually important. A small country's debt crisis can be painful and contagious. But distress in the central safe-asset structure would not merely pop one bubble. It would deform the foam.

**Q. Are smaller countries like smaller bubbles?**

Sometimes—but size turns out to be the least interesting variable.

A smaller country may have less fiscal depth, a smaller domestic investor base, greater exposure to foreign capital, and less ability to borrow in a global reserve currency. That can make its membrane thinner. But institutional credibility, currency denomination, maturity structure, productivity, demographics, political stability, current account position, and central bank credibility all matter more than volume.

A small bubble in the right structure can last.

A large bubble under the wrong pressure can deform.

Stability depends on the membrane and the surrounding foam, not on the amount of air inside.

## Can Bubbles Be Useful?

**Q. If bubbles can be dangerous, should they be avoided?**

Physical bubbles are not moral failures.

This is where the metaphor needs discipline. If "bubble" only means delusion, then the answer is easy: avoid bubbles. But a bubble in the physical sense is just a temporary structure produced by membranes, gases, pressure, and surface tension. Nothing about it is deceitful.

Likewise, not every expansion of credit is delusion. Not every use of debt is irresponsibility. Not every act of pulling the future into the present is wrong.

A civilization that refuses to borrow from the future may also refuse to build for the future.

The real question is what the borrowed future is used to create.

**Q. When is debt productive?**

Debt is productive when it increases the future capacity from which repayment must come.

If a government borrows to build infrastructure that raises productivity, educate people who later create value, finance research that expands technological capacity, or stabilize an economy during a temporary shock, debt can connect time in a defensible way. The future inherits both the burden and the asset.

In that case, debt is not merely a bubble.

It is a bridge.

**Q. When does debt become bubble-like?**

Debt becomes bubble-like when it preserves the present without strengthening the future.

If debt is used mainly to avoid political pain, postpone adjustment, hide structural weakness, or maintain consumption without increasing future capacity, then the membrane grows while the interior remains thin.

The numbers can still look official. The bonds can still trade. The institutions can still operate. The language can still sound responsible.

But the structure becomes more dependent on belief alone.

That is when public debt approaches the logic of a bubble.

Not because it is fake, but because its visible form has outrun the future that must support it.

**Q. Should controlled bubbles be encouraged?**

If a bubble is truly controlled, it may no longer be a bubble in the dangerous sense.

It may be credit. It may be investment. It may be fiscal policy. It may be insurance. It may be a managed risk.

The problem is that bubbles are rarely as controlled as they seem while they are expanding. Their membrane is made partly of human belief, and belief is not a mechanical material. It is affected by imitation, fear, politics, narratives, institutions, and the expectation of rescue.

So the better goal is not to encourage bubbles.

The better goal is to use debt without requiring delusion.

Borrowing can be justified. Fragility should not be romanticized. Trust can be used. Trust should not be abused.

A good society does not pretend it can live without membranes. It learns which membranes deserve maintenance.

## So What Is a Bubble, Really?

**Q. After all of this, can the question finally be answered in one sentence?**

Only if the sentence is allowed to change.

At the beginning, the answer was:

> A bubble is emptiness that has gained shape.

That remains true.

But the question expanded. A single bubble led to a membrane. The membrane led to fragility. Fragility led to trust. Trust led to debt. Debt led to public finance. Public finance led to global interdependence. And global interdependence changed the image from bubble to foam.

So the better answer is:

> A bubble is a fragile form created when a thin membrane gives shape to something that cannot hold that shape by itself.

That definition works for soap bubbles.

It also works, with caution, for economic bubbles. The membrane may be expectation. The interior may be future value. The form may be price.

For public debt, the metaphor must be adjusted:

> Public debt is not simply a bubble. It is the present shape of future obligation.

And for the global debt system:

> Public debt is not one giant bubble, but a foam of shared promises.

**Q. What did asking about bubbles actually reveal?**

That form does not always come from substance.

Sometimes form comes from a boundary. Sometimes a thing appears as one thing because a membrane holds together what would otherwise disperse.

This is true of soap bubbles. It is true of asset bubbles. It is true of public debt. It may also be true of institutions, reputations, identities, nations, and lives.

A person can appear coherent because memory forms a membrane around scattered moments. A nation can appear unified because law and ritual form a membrane around millions of separate lives. A market can appear rational because price forms a membrane around countless incompatible expectations.

The bubble is not an exception to reality.

It reveals something about reality.

Many things we call solid may be maintained forms. Many things we call stable may be membranes that have not yet torn.

**Q. Why does foam feel more modern than a single bubble?**

Because modern life is not made of isolated fragilities.

It is made of connected fragilities.

One person's debt is another person's asset. One country's obligation is another institution's reserve. One market's panic becomes another market's signal. One membrane's failure becomes another membrane's pressure.

The world does not simply burst.

It rearranges.

That may be more frightening than a single explosion. A burst bubble vanishes. Foam survives by redistributing its losses. It can continue after local collapse, but the continuation may depend on sacrificing weaker cells and strengthening stronger ones.

The danger, then, is not only that one bubble will pop.

The danger is that we do not know which membranes we are sharing.

**Q. Where does this leave the original image of the soap bubble?**

It remains necessary.

The child blowing soap bubbles sees something that adult language later covers with technical terms: surface tension, debt sustainability, asset prices, sovereign risk, financial contagion, refinancing, safe assets, restructuring.

But the child's image was not wrong.

A bubble is beautiful because it is temporary. It is visible because of a thin boundary. It moves lightly because it is almost empty. It can vanish because its form depends on conditions that are easy to disturb.

Modern public debt is not a soap bubble. It is more institutional, more durable, more useful, and more complex.

But it shares one philosophical structure with the bubble:

It makes the invisible visible.

Soap makes air visible as a sphere. Debt makes the future visible as a number. Foam makes interdependence visible as shared membranes.

None of this tells me which membranes I am standing on.

That is where the metaphor stops being useful. A soap bubble announces its own fragility—that is why a child can see it, chase it, and expect it to end. A membrane made of law, currency, tax capacity, and expectation announces nothing. It does not shimmer. It does not tremble in the air. It looks like ground.

So the question I am left with is not whether the form will hold.

It is how anyone, standing inside the foam, could tell the difference between a foundation and a surface that has simply not torn yet.

## Postscript: A Concept I Learned Afterward

This inquiry was written the way the others were written: by asking a question I actually had, in the order it actually occurred to me. The image of foam came from nowhere more distinguished than a thought that arrived as I was falling asleep. I had not read the economics literature on bubbles before writing, and I did not write toward a conclusion I already knew.

After finishing, I learned that a significant part of what I had worked out by metaphor already exists as a formal concept, and I want to record the overlap honestly rather than quietly absorb it.

The economist Hyman Minsky (1919–1996) proposed what he called the financial instability hypothesis, most compactly stated in a 1992 working paper. Minsky divided economic units into three financing postures according to the relationship between their debts and their cash flows:

- **Hedge finance** — the unit can meet all contractual obligations, interest and principal, out of its own cash flows.
- **Speculative finance** — the unit can meet interest payments out of income but cannot repay principal from it, and must therefore roll over its liabilities by issuing new debt to meet obligations on maturing debt.
- **Ponzi finance** — the unit cannot even meet interest commitments from cash flow, and depends on selling assets or borrowing further, typically sustained by the expectation that asset values will keep rising.

Minsky's second claim is the sharper one: over prolonged periods of prosperity, an economy tends to migrate from a structure dominated by hedge finance toward one weighted with speculative and Ponzi finance. Stability itself encourages the shift. The system does not require an external shock to become fragile; it manufactures its own fragility as a byproduct of calm.[^10]

Three things about this are worth stating plainly.

**First, the overlap is real and I did not invent it.** My distinction between debt that "enlarges the future it borrows from" and debt that "consumes the future before it arrives" is a metaphorical restatement of the boundary between hedge and Ponzi finance. My description of governments rolling over maturing debt also converges with Minsky's definition of a speculative unit. He discusses governments with floating debts in the same paragraph where he defines rollover-dependent speculative finance. I reached this structure by walking around the image of a membrane; he reached it by analyzing income-debt relations. The convergence is not to my credit, but it is not nothing either: it suggests the structure is visible from more than one entrance.

**Second, Minsky sharpens something the essay left blurred.** I wrote that "the belief that public debt can expand indefinitely without consequences can become a bubble," which locates the danger in belief. Minsky locates it somewhere less comfortable: in the ordinary, rational behavior of units that are individually behaving sensibly during good times. Nobody has to be deluded. The membrane thins because it is holding well. That is a harder claim than mine, and I think a better one.

**Third, the transfer to sovereign debt is contested, and I should not pretend otherwise.** Minsky was primarily analyzing private units in a capitalist economy—firms, banks, households. A government that issues debt in its own currency is not a firm, and economists disagree substantially about how far the taxonomy carries across. Some argue a currency-issuing sovereign can never be forced into a Ponzi posture in the way a company can; others argue the constraint reappears as inflation, currency depreciation, or political limits rather than default. I do not have a settled position on this, and the essay above does not resolve it. It should be read as an open question sitting underneath the whole middle section of this piece.

I am leaving this as a postscript rather than folding it into the body because the body is a record of how I actually got here, and revising it to look better-read than I was would distort that record. The point of this series is the path, not the arrival.
