> Modern Philosophy begins not with grand theories, but with ordinary things we have seen all our lives and never properly questioned.

*A dated, revisable archive of inquiry by Hyunyong Jung.*

*First published July 31, 2026 · Last revised July 31, 2026*

Most things become hardest to explain after they become familiar.

We use words such as *oil*, *water*, *fire*, *money*, and *intelligence* without much hesitation. They work well enough in ordinary life. But the moment I try to define one precisely—what belongs inside the category, what does not, and why—the apparent simplicity begins to break apart.

Modern Philosophy is my attempt to stay with that moment rather than move past it.

---

## How It Began

This project did not begin with a plan to publish philosophy.

The idea emerged from a conversation about returning to childhood with my present memories. Could I design a better life without losing the relationships that matter to me now? That question became a discussion about relationships, then control, uncertainty, risk, gambling, prediction, and the desire to be right before everyone else.

What stayed with me was not one conclusion, but the path between the questions: why an answer made another question necessary, where an explanation stopped satisfying me, and how a thought experiment exposed assumptions I had never stated directly.

I wanted a place to preserve those paths rather than let them disappear inside private conversations.

## Why Oil Became #001

At the same time, I had been carrying a much more ordinary question: **What is oil, exactly?**

Cooking oil, animal fat, petroleum, motor oil, and the oil on human skin are all described by the same word. Yet they differ in origin, chemical structure, purpose, and behavior. What do they actually share? Is oil one substance, a family of substances, or a category built from similarities we notice on the surface?

That question unsettled several other words I thought I understood. Is water the opposite of oil? Why do the two separate? What is an emulsion? What makes something a fuel? Why does burning release heat? How have oils shaped food, bodies, transportation, industry, and culture?

A narrow question about a familiar material opened into chemistry, biology, cooking, energy, history, economics, language, and classification. Each answer made another question necessary.

At first, I called this movement a *rabbit hole*. The phrase described the branching, but not the reason for following it. I was not collecting trivia. I was testing concepts that familiarity had made invisible and refusing to stop when the first answer only appeared sufficient.

That was close to what philosophy had come to mean to me. Oil was the first subject through which the method became fully visible, giving the project both its form and its name.

## What “Modern Philosophy” Means

The title does not mean that this project surveys the historical period conventionally called modern philosophy. It is not a guide to canonical philosophers or established schools of thought.

Here, **modern** describes the world from which the questions arise: its materials, technologies, institutions, habits, and forms of life. **Philosophy** describes the practice: questioning what is treated as obvious, separating things that only appear identical, and revising a concept when its boundaries no longer hold.

The subjects may seem unrelated, but breadth is not the organizing principle. The project is held together by a recurring act: taking a word, object, behavior, or system that works smoothly in ordinary life and asking what must be true for it to make sense.

Oil can become a question about categories and language. Public debt can become a question about money and obligation. Artificial intelligence can become a question about intelligence, memory, judgment, and value.

The philosophy lies not in forcing every subject to sound profound. It lies in taking an ordinary question seriously enough for its hidden structure to become visible.

## How an Inquiry Works

Every inquiry begins with a question I genuinely had. Topics are not chosen to fit a fixed category or promise a neat conclusion. I begin without a predetermined outline and follow the question where it goes.

Most inquiries develop through extended dialogue with AI. The first answer is a proposal, not a conclusion. I ask for distinctions, press on vague language, challenge weak examples, and look for exceptions or contradictions. Sometimes the explanation changes. Sometimes the question changes. Sometimes the premise from which I began turns out to be wrong.

Only after the inquiry has developed far enough does it become a piece of writing. Factual claims are checked against sources. Unsupported statements are removed, corrected, or qualified. Repeated exchanges are condensed, the sequence is reorganized, and both questions and answers are rewritten for publication.

The final pieces remain in continuous Q&A form. They are not raw transcripts: rough phrasing, repetition, errors, and conversational debris are removed. But they are not conventional essays that erase the questions and present the final explanation as though it appeared fully formed.

A serious follow-up question records a limit in the answer before it. It shows where a definition became unstable, where an exception mattered, or where a new distinction was needed.

> The purpose is not only to communicate what was learned. It is to preserve how understanding moved.

Many inquiries begin in Korean, where the original questions are most immediate to me. The English publication is not a line-by-line translation, but a new editorial version that preserves the logic and movement of the inquiry in natural English.

## The Role of AI

I use AI as an interlocutor, not as an authority.

It can propose explanations, counterarguments, terminology, and directions for research. It can also be incomplete, misleading, or confidently wrong. Its answers are material for examination, never evidence by themselves.

I remain responsible for the questions, objections, judgments, sources, and final wording. I do not publish raw transcripts or accept a response as a conclusion merely because it is fluent.

Yet I do not erase the dialogic origin of the work. The friction between a proposed answer and my refusal to accept it too quickly is part of the method. AI provides something to test; the project takes shape in what happens after the answer arrives.

## A Philosophy Still Taking Shape

Modern Philosophy is neither an encyclopedia nor a course in philosophy. It is a dated, revisable archive of sustained inquiry.

Each piece records when it was first published, when it was last revised, and which sources support its factual claims. The aim is not to make early thoughts look permanent, but to make their development visible and accountable.

I am not beginning with a finished philosophical system and dividing it into essays. The order is the reverse. If a personal philosophy emerges, it should be earned slowly from the questions themselves.

Over time, recurring concerns may become visible: what makes different things members of the same category; where familiar definitions fail; how humans assign value; what we mean by intelligence; why control and uncertainty can attract us at once; and how ordinary words conceal structures we rarely notice.

Publishing turns private curiosity into a cumulative record. It makes it possible to see not only what I thought, but how that way of thinking changed.

Oil will be the first published inquiry—not because it is the most important subject, but because it was the first familiar thing through which the method of this project became clear.

**Modern Philosophy will continue wherever an ordinary thing stops being ordinary.**

— **Hyunyong Jung**
